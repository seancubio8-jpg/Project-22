How the SmartSip water dispenser is designed and built

A proposed Arduino-controlled dispenser that monitors the water supply and dispenses measured portions. This guide explains the concept, suggested hardware, connections, assembly sequence, and operating cycle.

Important project status: The companion SmartSip website is a browser simulator. It demonstrates the control logic, but it does not connect to physical Arduino hardware. The parts and construction steps below describe a suggested physical prototype that would need to be assembled, programmed, calibrated, and tested separately.

1. Project overview

SmartSip is designed to make water dispensing more controlled and convenient. A microcontroller reads the water level in a reservoir. A user chooses a portion size and starts dispensing. The controller operates a low-voltage pump, while a flow sensor can measure the amount delivered. A display can show the water level and system status.

Main goal Monitor the tank and dispense a selected amount of water with a simple user interface.

Have the wiring and enclosure checked by a qualified instructor or technician before powering a physical build.

4. System architecture

The physical prototype can be divided into four parts:

Input section

Water-level sensor, flow sensor, and push buttons send readings or commands to the Arduino.

Controller section

The Arduino checks the tank level, reads the selected portion, counts flow pulses, and decides when to stop.

Output section

A MOSFET driver or relay switches the DC pump. An optional LCD displays the current state.

Water path

Reservoir → pump → food-grade tubing → flow sensor → outlet nozzle → cup. Exact order depends on the pump and sensor manufacturer's instructions.

A flow sensor is recommended for measured portions. A timed pump run alone only estimates volume and can vary with pump, head height, and battery/supply voltage.

6. Step-by-step construction process

1

Plan the layout

Choose a stable base and reservoir position. Mark separate dry and wet zones. Decide where the pump, sensor, outlet, buttons, and optional LCD will fit.

2

Prepare the water container

Clean the food-grade reservoir. Make secure openings or fittings for the pump intake, tubing, and sensor mount as required by the design. Prevent sharp edges and leaks.

3

Build the water path

Connect the reservoir, pump, flow sensor, tubing, and nozzle according to the component instructions. Secure the tubing and check that water cannot drip onto electronics.

4

Mount the electronics

Install the Arduino, pump driver, button wiring, and optional display inside a dry enclosure. Keep connectors protected and provide strain relief for cables.

5

Connect the sensors

Wire the level sensor and flow sensor to the chosen input pins. Check supply voltage, signal levels, polarity, and the manufacturer's pin labels before powering up.

6

Connect the pump driver

Wire the DC pump through a properly rated MOSFET or relay driver and its separate supply. Add the required fuse and inductive-load protection specified by the design.

7

Upload and configure the program

Write or upload Arduino firmware to read the sensors, handle the buttons, count flow pulses, drive the pump, and stop at the selected volume or a low-water condition.

8

Calibrate the readings

Compare flow-sensor pulse counts with a measured container of water. Determine the pulses-per-millilitre value for the actual sensor and pump setup. Calibrate the tank-level readings too.

9

Test safely

Test the electronics first without water, then test for leaks with the electronics isolated. Verify stop behavior, low-water protection, and repeatability using small amounts of water.

10

Enclose and document

After testing, secure the wiring, close the dry enclosure, label the buttons and power input, and document the final pin map and calibration values.

7. How the dispenser operates

  1. Power on: The Arduino initializes the sensors, buttons, pump driver, and optional display.
  2. Check tank: The level sensor estimates how much water remains. Dispensing should be disabled if the tank is too low.
  3. Select a portion: The user selects a target amount, for example 100 mL, 250 mL, or 500 mL.
  4. Start dispensing: The controller activates the pump driver and monitors flow-sensor pulses.
  5. Stop at target: When the calibrated pulse count reaches the target volume, the controller switches the pump off.
  6. Update status: The display reports the amount dispensed and remaining tank level.
  7. Stop or refill: The user can stop a cycle. If the tank is low or empty, the system should alert the user and prevent dry running.

These are proposed physical-system behaviors. The current website simulates portion dispensing and tank-level changes; it does not read real sensors or drive a real pump.

9. Testing checklist

  • Confirm the water-level reading at full, half-full, and low levels.
  • Confirm the pump cannot start when the tank is below the safe threshold.
  • Measure the delivered volume for each selected portion.
  • Check that Stop immediately disables the pump driver.
  • Check for leaks and ensure the electronics compartment remains dry.
  • Repeat tests and record calibration values and any errors.
